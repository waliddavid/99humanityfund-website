# What the Fund Is — and What It Isn't

Most readers, after finishing Part 1, will mentally file the fund into a category that already exists. Some will think of it as a charity that pays dividends. Some will think of it as a sovereign wealth fund without a country. Some will think of it as a giant mutual fund. Some will think of it as a hedge fund for poor people, or a cooperative scaled up.

None of these categories quite fit. The differences sound technical but they matter — because each existing category has serious limitations the fund is specifically designed to overcome. Walking through the comparisons explains why none of these existing institutions ever did what the fund proposes to do.

## The fund is not a sovereign wealth fund

Norway's Government Pension Fund Global is probably the closest existing institution to what people imagine when they hear "global investment fund for citizens." It owns about 1.5% of every listed company in the world, generates returns that go into Norway's national budget, and is celebrated as a model of how a country can use natural resource wealth wisely. Norway's fund has assets exceeding \$1.7 trillion as of early 2026 — roughly \$300,000 per Norwegian citizen.

But notice what that institution is: it's *Norway's* fund. It belongs to the Norwegian state. It exists because oil revenue in the 1990s went into the fund rather than into general government spending. Only Norwegian citizens benefit. The fund's returns flow to the Norwegian government, which then uses them to fund Norwegian state services. A Vietnamese worker has no claim on it, even though some of the companies Norway's fund owns operate in Vietnam and profit from Vietnamese labor.

Abu Dhabi, Singapore, Saudi Arabia, and China all have similar funds. Each serves its own state's interests. None operate as global institutions. None pay dividends to humans regardless of citizenship. None compete with private companies on price or labor practices — they're passive investors that maximize return on capital, not active operators that pursue social objectives.

The fund is what sovereign wealth funds would have been if they had been built for citizens of the world rather than citizens of specific countries. It has no national flag, no government parent, no strategic objective beyond serving its members equally everywhere.

## The fund is not an index fund

Vanguard and BlackRock collectively manage about \$20 trillion in assets. Most of that money belongs to ordinary people — retirees, families saving for college, workers in 401(k) plans. By any honest accounting, hundreds of millions of people already "own" small slices of the world's largest companies through index funds.

So a fair question is: if index funds already exist and already democratize ownership of corporations, why do we need a fund?

The answer is that index funds democratize *ownership* but not *control*. When you own a share of an S&P 500 index fund, the fund holds voting rights on your behalf — but in practice, Vanguard and BlackRock vote almost entirely with corporate management on every meaningful issue. They are passive investors by design. Their stated philosophy is that they shouldn't impose their views on the companies they own; they should simply maximize returns within the rules.

The result: index funds own roughly 25% of the US stock market, and they have used that ownership to change essentially nothing about how corporations operate. Profit margins are at historic highs. Worker pay has stagnated. Executive compensation continues to climb. Environmental practices are largely unchanged. Why? Because the largest "owners" don't actually use their ownership to steer the companies. They just collect dividends and report performance.

The fund is the opposite. Its entire purpose is active ownership. It steers the companies it owns — toward fair wages, regional manufacturing, capped margins on essentials, transparent operations. Index funds are owners in name only. The fund is an owner in fact.

There's also a participation difference. To buy a Vanguard fund you typically need a bank account, internet access, often a minimum investment, and enough financial literacy to know what an index fund is. About 1.4 billion adults globally have no bank account. None of them participate in index funds. The fund is designed for them too.

## The fund is not a hedge fund or private equity firm

A hedge fund pools money from wealthy individuals and institutions and pursues high returns through aggressive trading, leverage, or specialized strategies. A private equity firm buys companies, often using debt, and tries to increase their value before reselling at a profit. Both serve their investors — who, almost without exception, are wealthy.

The fund is not optimized for return. It's optimized for *who benefits*. It accepts lower returns if those returns flow to everyone equally rather than higher returns flowing to a few. It accepts longer time horizons — measured in generations, not quarters — because its members aren't trying to time the market. It accepts lower-margin operations on essential goods because consumer welfare matters more than fund profits.

Hedge funds would never operate this way. They couldn't — their investors expect returns competitive with the market. The fund's "investors" expect something different: that the fund exists, that it pays them a meaningful dividend, and that it operates as a counterweight to concentrated corporate power. The fund's success is measured not in maximum returns but in how many people receive how much, and what that does to the economies they live in.

## The fund is not a charity

This is worth saying clearly because some readers will pattern-match the fund to GiveDirectly or Oxfam or other organizations that collect money from people of means and distribute it to people in need. The model is fundamentally different.

A charity collects donations and gives them away. The money flows in one direction — from donors to recipients — and is consumed in the process. The donors don't get anything back materially; the recipients don't accumulate ownership of anything.

The fund collects contributions and *invests* them. Members are not donors and not recipients; they are co-owners. Their \$1 a month buys them a share of a growing pool of productive assets that generates returns indefinitely. The dividend is not charity — it's a share of profits from companies the member partially owns. A person in Bangladesh who contributes \$1 a month for 20 years isn't receiving charity from wealthy donors. They are receiving the return on capital they themselves contributed, alongside billions of others doing the same.

The donation fund mentioned in Part 1 — where people of means can donate to cover the \$1 contribution for people who can't afford it — is the only charitable element of the structure. But notice what it does: it converts donations into permanent ownership stakes for the people who couldn't otherwise participate. A donor's \$12 a year buys someone a permanent share of the global economy.

Crucially, the donation is time-limited per recipient. Once the recipient's accumulated dividends grow large enough to cover their own \$1 a month, they begin funding themselves from their share of fund profits. The donor's role ends; the recipient becomes a self-sustaining member. This is structurally different from traditional charity in two ways: the donation produces permanent ownership rather than temporary consumption, and the donation flow tapers off naturally as recipients become economically self-sufficient. A donor doesn't have to keep giving forever to keep the recipient afloat. The fund itself eventually replaces the donor.

## The fund is closest to a global Mondragón

In 1956, in the Basque town of Mondragón in northern Spain, a Catholic priest named José María Arizmendiarrieta started a worker cooperative making paraffin heaters. Today, that experiment has grown into the Mondragón Corporation — a federation of 95 cooperatives employing over 70,000 people, generating €11 billion in annual revenue, operating in finance, retail, manufacturing, and technology, with its own university and its own bank.

Mondragón cooperatives are owned by their workers, not by external shareholders. Decisions are made democratically: one worker, one vote, regardless of seniority. Profits are reinvested in the cooperatives, distributed among workers, or contributed to community development. When economic downturns hit, workers are transferred between cooperatives rather than laid off. When new ventures launch, the cooperative bank provides patient capital.

Mondragón is not a thought experiment. It's a working institution, sixty-nine years old, operating in markets and competing with conventional capitalist firms. It hasn't taken over the Spanish economy or even the Basque economy, but it has proven that democratically-owned competitive enterprises can survive and grow at scale.

The fund applies Mondragón's principles to a different unit. Instead of "the workers in this factory own this company" the principle becomes "every human being on earth owns one share of the global productive system." The structure scales from a regional federation of workers to a planetary federation of citizens.

There are real differences. Mondragón members are typically workers in Mondragón cooperatives; their stake is tied to their labor. The fund members are humans, regardless of whether they work in fund-owned companies or not. Mondragón decisions happen in physical assemblies in the Basque Country. The fund decisions happen digitally across 194 countries. Mondragón has built itself over seven decades; the fund proposes to build itself in three.

These differences make the fund a more ambitious experiment. They don't make it implausible. What Mondragón shows is that citizen ownership at significant scale is not utopian fantasy — it's been operating in northern Spain for two-thirds of a century. What the fund proposes is that the same principle, technologically enabled and globally federated, can serve everyone.

## Why this matters for the rest of Part 2

Each of the next six articles addresses a specific question — what the fund owns, how it competes, how the money flows, how it gets built, what stops it from being corrupted, what the first decade looks like. But all of those questions assume an institutional category that doesn't quite exist in the world yet. The fund is its own category.

When you read about how the fund competes with private companies in Article 2-4, you can't think of it as a hedge fund fighting for returns or an index fund passively along for the ride. You have to think of it as something new: a permanent, citizen-owned, mission-driven, democratically-governed federation of productive enterprises, competing with private capital on grounds where its structural advantages compound over decades.

When you read about governance in Article 2-7, you can't think of it as a corporation's board or a charity's trustees or a sovereign wealth fund's investment committee. You have to think of it as a global federation of co-owners, with all the deliberative complexity that implies.

<p class="closing-call">The fund is not any of the things you already know. That's the point. The world doesn't yet have the institution we need. Part 2 is about whether we can build it.</p>
