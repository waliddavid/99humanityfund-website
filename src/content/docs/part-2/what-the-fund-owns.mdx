# What the Fund Owns and How That Grows

The most common misconception about the fund is that it tries to own everything. It doesn't. It owns specific things in a specific order, for specific reasons, and there are entire categories of economic activity it deliberately enters last or not at all.

This article walks through what the fund actually holds across three decades — what it acquires first, what it acquires later, what it leaves to private capital indefinitely. The numbers in Part 1's dividend math don't make sense without this picture. A reader who finishes Part 1 thinking "the fund will eventually own the Forbes Global 2000" has only half the story. The interesting question isn't whether the fund grows. It's *which* industries it enters, in *what order*, and *why*.

## The first principle: one company per industry

The fund's design rule, stated in Part 1, is one company per industry. Not all the companies. Not zero companies. One.

That number is doing real work. If the fund owned zero companies in a given industry, it would have no influence on that industry's wages, prices, supply chains, or labor practices. The fund would be a passive investor — exactly what index funds already are and what we've established doesn't change anything.

If the fund owned all the companies in a given industry, it would be a monopoly. Worse, it would be a *democratically governed* monopoly, which sounds appealing until you remember that democratically governed monopolies still suppress innovation, still develop bureaucratic inertia, and still concentrate power even when the power is held collectively. Mondragón doesn't own all the cooperatives in the Basque Country. It owns 95 of them, alongside private competitors. That's the principle.

One company per industry means the fund is a meaningful competitor — large enough that its wage policies, pricing decisions, and labor practices set a floor that private competitors have to respond to — without becoming the sole provider. Consumers still have choice. Workers can still leave fund companies for private ones, or the reverse. Innovation still comes from many sources. The fund is the participant that pulls the industry toward better outcomes, not the participant that defines all outcomes.

In practice, "one company" might mean a single corporate entity at fund-sized scale, or it might mean a cluster of smaller fund-owned firms operating in the same industry. The principle is about market share: somewhere in the range of 15-30% of an industry, not 70% and not 5%. Enough to matter. Not enough to dominate.

## Wave 1: essentials and financial infrastructure (Years 1-8)

The fund's first wave runs on two parallel tracks. Both have to happen at once because neither works without the other.

**Track A: Essential goods.** Essential goods are the natural entry point for the fund. Food, basic medicine, water utilities, basic consumer staples — these share three properties that make them ideal for fund entry.

First, demand is stable. People buy food and medicine in good economies and bad. Fund-owned essentials companies generate consistent cash flow that compounds into capital for the next acquisition.

Second, consumer preference for fund-owned products is strongest in essentials. A person choosing between fund-owned bread and private bread is choosing where their grocery money goes when they're already spending it. The dividend feedback loop works most clearly here: more spending on fund products produces more fund profits which produce larger dividends which produce more reason for consumers to choose fund products, and the cycle repeats.

Third, the political case for the fund's existence is most defensible in essentials. "We exist to make medicine affordable" is a winning political argument that survives most attacks. "We exist to make luxury handbags affordable" isn't — that conversation comes later, and in a very different framing.

The implied Wave 1 essential goods portfolio:

- Basic pharmaceuticals (generic drugs, essential medicines on WHO lists)
- Staple food production and processing (grains, oils, dairy, basic packaged foods)
- Water and basic utilities, where local regulation permits private operators
- Basic housing materials (cement, steel for housing, basic fixtures)
- Affordable consumer staples (soap, basic hygiene products, household basics)

These industries share another property: many existing competitors, often consolidated into a few large players, but no single firm at unreachable scale. The fund can acquire one of those existing players, or build a new entrant, without immediately dominating any industry. With cumulative contributions plus reinvested profits, the fund has the capital to enter these markets meaningfully within the first decade.

**Track B: Financial infrastructure.** This track is less obvious to readers but possibly more important. The fund cannot operate without its own financial backbone, and that backbone has to exist from day one.

Here's the threat the fund faces. International finance currently runs on infrastructure dominated by US institutions: the SWIFT messaging system, the dollar-denominated correspondent banking network, and US Treasury's OFAC sanctions regime. If the fund grows large enough to be perceived as a threat to US financial primacy, the US can arbitrarily add the fund's entities to its sanctions list, pressure SWIFT to disconnect them, and pressure correspondent banks worldwide to refuse fund accounts. About 88% of international transactions touch the dollar at some point. The fund cannot exist as a serious institution if its existence depends on the goodwill of a single government's financial infrastructure.

The defensive answer is to build fund-owned financial infrastructure before it's needed. Specifically: a fund-owned digital bank in each country where the fund operates, headquartered in that country, regulated by that country's central bank, fully compliant with local law.

The model is similar to how Nu Bank entered Colombia and Brazil. They started with a single product — a credit card — operating purely digitally with no physical branches. After demonstrating safety and gaining regulatory trust, they expanded into savings accounts, then small loans at competitive rates, then a full digital banking license. The fund could follow the same path: enter each country with a single product (likely savings accounts and small loans), build a customer base and regulatory standing, then expand into broader financial services.

This approach raises money where it already lives. A member in Indonesia contributes \$1 per month to the fund's Indonesian digital bank. That money stays in Indonesia, denominated in rupiah, until the fund needs to deploy it. There's no need for a daily cross-border transfer that could be blocked. Funds move internationally only when an acquisition requires it, and only between countries friendly to fund operations.

It also makes the fund difficult to shut down. If any government tries to ban fund operations, that government would have to ban a popular consumer bank that pays competitive rates and serves people the major banks won't serve. The political cost of doing that — angering millions of customers whose financial life depends on the fund's bank — is high enough that even hostile governments would hesitate. The fund's first defense against political attack is its own customers.

## How the bank actually operates: participation rather than interest extraction

The fund's bank operates on a fundamentally different model than conventional banks. This is worth explaining because it shapes everything else the bank does.

Conventional banks earn most of their profit from the spread between what they pay depositors (very little) and what they charge borrowers (substantially more). A US retail bank might pay 0.5% on savings while charging 7% on personal loans, 22% on credit cards, and 12% on mortgages. The spread is the structural source of banking profit. It isn't compensation for productive work — banks don't grow grain or build chairs. It's compensation for the privilege of operating in the middle of the money flow.

Worse, banks don't lend their own money. They lend depositor money, and through fractional reserve banking they lend many times more than they actually hold. When a bank issues a loan, it creates new deposit money on its balance sheet that didn't exist before. A homeowner paying \$500,000 in interest over 30 years on a \$400,000 mortgage isn't paying for the use of someone else's \$400,000 — they're paying \$500,000 of their lifetime productive labor to the bank in exchange for the bank's permission to live in a house. This dynamic, over generations, is one of the most powerful mechanisms in modern economics for concentrating wealth upward.

The fund's mission is to redistribute power and reduce extractive transactions. Operating a conventional interest-spread bank would contradict that mission with one hand while the dividends try to fix it with the other. So the fund's bank uses a different model.

*Savings accounts share the bank's actual profits.* Members deposit money with the fund's bank. The bank doesn't pay predetermined interest. Instead, depositors receive a share of the bank's actual profits from its lending and investment activities. The bank targets returns equivalent to local inflation plus a modest premium, but the payments are structured as profit-sharing rather than guaranteed interest. In good years, depositors earn more; in difficult years, they earn less. The bank's performance and what depositors receive are transparently connected.

*Lending to businesses takes the form of equity investment with buyback rights.* When a small business needs capital to start or grow, the bank doesn't issue an interest-bearing loan. The bank evaluates the business, performs proper risk assessment, and invests as a partner. The bank takes an equity stake proportional to the capital provided and shares in profits and losses going forward. The founder can buy back the bank's stake at any time at the business's market value, recovering full ownership. If the business fails, the bank loses its investment alongside the founder. If it thrives, the bank earns its return through dividends and buyback proceeds. This is what venture capital does for high-growth startups; the fund's innovation is to apply this model to ordinary small businesses that traditionally take on interest-bearing debt.

*Fund-aligned businesses receive zero-cost capital.* If a local business sources from fund-owned suppliers — a restaurant buying from fund-owned food producers, a clinic stocking fund-owned generic pharmaceuticals, a construction firm using fund-owned cement — the fund's bank provides capital without seeking direct return. The bank's "return" comes indirectly, through additional revenue flowing to fund-owned suppliers downstream. This creates a self-reinforcing ecosystem: cheap capital draws aligned businesses, aligned businesses route demand to fund-owned suppliers, suppliers grow profits, profits return as dividends.

*The bank operates investor marketplaces.* Members can directly fund other members' business ventures through the bank's platform. The bank verifies businesses, performs due diligence, and provides legal infrastructure, but the primary capital comes from willing members. The bank may co-invest alongside members on terms that signal its confidence, but it doesn't take all the risk. This model is similar to how equity crowdfunding works, operating inside the fund's ecosystem with fund members as the capital pool.

*Operating costs are covered by productive activity, not by interest spread.* The bank earns from its equity investments when businesses succeed. It earns transaction fees on payment processing, currency conversion, and remittances. It earns from its card network operations (described below). It earns modest service fees on specialized banking services like financial advisory or business account management. Unlike a conventional standalone bank that must be profitable on interest spread alone, the fund's bank operates within the broader fund — its costs are covered by productive returns rather than by extracting value from borrowers.

Taken together, these banking products produce a structural distinction worth naming clearly. **The fund competes with global businesses and supports local businesses.** The fund's competitive force is directed at the multinational corporations and financial institutions that currently extract value from consumers worldwide. The fund's enabling force is directed at the local entrepreneurs, small businesses, restaurants, clinics, workshops, and family enterprises that are the actual economic life of communities everywhere. A local restaurant sourcing from fund-owned food producers gets cheaper capital than competitors that don't. A local clinic stocking fund-owned generics can offer lower prices to its patients. A family workshop using fund-owned cement and basic materials can compete with imported alternatives. The fund grows by competing upward against concentrated corporate power; it grows the broader economy by enabling outward across millions of local enterprises that are part of its ecosystem. This distinction matters because the populist critique of "global institutions destroying local economies" is exactly inverted in the fund's design — the fund is the global institution that *strengthens* local economies, by redirecting the value currently captured by multinational corporations back into communities where people actually live and work.

## The card network: a fund-owned alternative to Visa and Mastercard

Part of the bank's Wave 1 build-out is a global card network competing with Visa and Mastercard. The case for this is strong on multiple grounds.

Currently when a consumer pays with a credit card, the merchant doesn't receive the full sale price. The fee is split among issuing banks (1.5-2.5% in the US, 0.3% in the EU after their interchange cap), card networks (about 0.15%), and payment processors (0.3-1%). Merchants typically pay 2-4% per transaction in the US, less in regulated markets. Globally this aggregates to hundreds of billions of dollars per year flowing from merchants — and ultimately from consumers through baked-in prices — to a handful of US-controlled payment infrastructure companies.

A fund-owned card network charges merchants a substantially lower fee, perhaps 1% total. This is operationally feasible — India's UPI handles 11 billion transactions per month at essentially zero merchant cost; Brazil's Pix operates similarly. The technology exists; what hasn't existed is a global network operating with the fund's incentive structure rather than profit maximization.

At meaningful scale, the economics work cleanly. If the fund's card network reaches 5% of global card volume by Year 15 — perhaps \$1.5 trillion annually — and charges 1% instead of 3%, it generates \$15 billion in revenue covering operations, fraud prevention, and infrastructure, while saving merchants \$30 billion. Those merchant savings flow into lower consumer prices.

The strategic value extends beyond the direct economics. **A fund-owned card network gives the fund operational independence from US-controlled payment infrastructure.** Visa and Mastercard rails can be cut off by political pressure; the fund's own rails cannot. The card network is part of the same financial-autonomy strategy as the digital banks.

For consumer convenience, the fund's card offers installment payment options structured as deferred-sale transactions rather than interest-bearing loans. Short installments (up to 90 days) are provided free as a card benefit, financed by merchant fees on those transactions. Longer installments use a different structure: when the consumer chooses to pay over time, the bank effectively purchases the item and resells it to the consumer at a fixed total price, payable in monthly installments. The total price is known upfront; there is no compounding; lateness produces administrative fees but does not grow the principal. The markup over the cash price reflects the bank's cost of capital and inflation protection, calibrated by purchase category — minimal for essentials (medical care, education, basic appliances), market rate for discretionary purchases (consumer electronics, travel, luxury goods).

For larger productive purchases — a small business buying equipment, a family acquiring an income-generating asset — the bank offers a profit-sharing alternative. The bank co-owns the asset with the borrower and receives a percentage of the income it generates for a defined period, capped at a total return. When the cap is reached or the period ends, ownership transfers fully to the borrower. This is structurally identical to the equity investment model used for new businesses, just extended to asset financing.

Two tracks together produce Wave 1: by Year 8, the fund operates digital banks across dozens of countries, runs a global card network at fair merchant fees, holds meaningful share in essential goods industries, and has accumulated enough reinvested profit to fund the next wave's acquisitions. Members have started receiving dividends. The fund has demonstrated that it works as a real institution.

## The margin trajectory: how the fund expands during Wave 1

A common question at this point: where does the capital for further acquisitions come from? Member contributions of \$1 a month, even at 8 billion members, produce only about \$96 billion a year. Major acquisitions cost more than that. So how does the fund grow faster than its contribution income would allow?

The answer is the margin trajectory.

The fund's long-term policy, established in Part 1, is to cap profit margins at 10% on industries where current margins exceed that threshold. But the fund doesn't immediately compress margins on acquisition. It does so gradually, and uses the difference between competitor-margin and fund-target-margin to fund expansion during the transition.

Here's how this works in practice. Suppose the fund acquires a company in an industry currently operating at 20% net profit margins. The acquired company generates, say, \$10 billion a year in profit. The fund's dividend policy distributes profits equivalent to the eventual 10% margin level — so members receive dividends as if the company were earning 5 billion. The remaining \$5 billion is reinvested into expansion: acquiring more companies, building infrastructure, supporting fund operations.

In Year 2, the fund lowers the company's margin to 19%. Consumer prices drop slightly. Profits fall to \$9.5 billion. The fund still distributes dividends at the 10% equivalent (\$5 billion). The reinvestment pool from this company is \$4.5 billion.

In Year 3, margin drops to 18%. Profits \$9 billion. Dividends still \$5 billion. Reinvestment \$4 billion.

The fund continues this trajectory for ten years. By Year 11, the company's margin is at 10%. Profits are \$5 billion. Dividends are \$5 billion. There is no additional reinvestment from this company — but by now, the fund has used the cumulative reinvestment from the prior decade to acquire additional companies, each of which generates its own margin-trajectory reinvestment stream.

The compounding effect is significant. A company that would have produced \$10 billion a year in dividends to private shareholders produces, in this model, about \$50 billion in reinvestment across the ten-year transition. That \$50 billion buys other companies, which produce their own reinvestment streams, which buy yet more companies. The fund's expansion is fueled by the gap between current industry margins and its eventual margin target.

Three things to notice about this design.

First, **members feel no dividend cut during the transition**. They receive dividends from day one at the level the fund will eventually sustain at full operation. The reinvestment is invisible to them because it's coming from a margin reduction they wouldn't have seen anyway.

Second, **consumer prices fall gradually rather than suddenly**. A 1% margin reduction per year is barely perceptible to consumers. Private competitors have time to adjust, governments don't panic, supply chains don't shock. The fund's mission of lowering essential prices proceeds without triggering an emergency response from anyone.

Third, **the fund self-funds its expansion** during precisely the years when it most needs capital. Wave 1 is the period when the fund has to acquire many companies quickly to establish credibility and scale. The margin trajectory provides the cash flow that makes this possible.

One caveat: this only works for industries where the fund acquires a company operating at higher-than-target margins. For industries already at 10% or below — commodity manufacturing, basic consumer goods with thin margins — there's no margin compression and no extra reinvestment stream. The fund operates at industry norm from day one. This is one reason why entering high-margin industries early (essentials, basic pharma) is strategically important: those are the industries that generate the reinvestment that funds entry into the lower-margin ones.

## Wave 2: labor-intensive industries and healthcare services (Years 8-18)

With Wave 1 cash flow and the margin-trajectory reinvestment compounding, the fund enters labor-intensive industries and healthcare services.

Labor-intensive industries are where the manufacturing redistribution from Part 3 becomes operational:

- Textiles and apparel manufacturing
- Furniture and home goods
- Consumer electronics assembly (not chip design — just assembly)
- Vehicle parts and basic automotive components
- Processed food at scale
- Basic chemicals and plastics

These industries employ tens of millions of workers globally. The fund acquiring or building companies here directly translates into the Informal Insertion scenario from Part 3 of this series: the formalization of informal workers and the regional redistribution of manufacturing employment. Wave 2 is when the worker-redistribution argument becomes a lived experience for the workers involved, not an abstract proposal.

The capital requirements rise here. A major textile manufacturer or vehicle parts company can cost \$20-80 billion. By Year 15, with cumulative contributions plus reinvested fund profits, the fund has the capital. But the operational complexity rises too. The fund needs experienced operators, supply chain managers, and engineers to run companies at this scale. The fund's hiring practices matter, and the fund's ability to attract top talent at competitive market salaries becomes essential.

Alongside labor-intensive manufacturing, **healthcare services** enters Wave 2.

Pharma and healthcare are intertwined. The fund's Wave 1 entry into basic pharmaceuticals already lowers drug prices in fund-served markets. Healthcare services — clinics, outpatient care, basic hospitals, integrated care delivery — is the next logical step. Lower drug prices plus lower care costs together produce a meaningful reduction in household healthcare expenditure, which in turn frees up family income for other spending.

The healthcare execution depends heavily on the existing structure in each country. Colombia's two-tier system is a useful template. Colombia operates a mandatory universal layer (the EPS contributory regime) where everyone pays into a public-private health insurance system based on income. On top of that, an optional private layer (medicina prepagada) competes for customers willing to pay more for faster access, better facilities, and broader networks. The two layers complement each other — the EPS guarantees coverage; the prepagada provides choice.

In countries with strong universal baseline coverage (Colombia, Brazil, most of Europe, the UK, Japan, Australia), the fund operates on the supplementary layer — the prepagada equivalent. It competes with private supplementary insurers, offering coverage at fairer terms, accepting pre-existing conditions over longer payback horizons, and integrating with fund-owned hospitals and clinics. The fund's digital bank from Wave 1 becomes the natural mechanism for collecting monthly premiums and routing them to fund-owned providers.

In countries without strong universal baseline coverage (the United States most prominently, parts of Asia and Africa, much of the Middle East), the fund operates as primary coverage — competing with private insurers directly, offering more affordable comprehensive plans, leveraging the same banking-and-provider integration that operates elsewhere in supplementary mode.

What the fund does *not* do is try to replace government-run universal health systems. Those exist for good reasons, they're popular where they exist, and competing with them would invite political resistance the fund doesn't need. The fund builds where private capital already operates — and undercuts that private capital with citizen-owned alternatives that integrate with the rest of the member's relationship with the fund.

## Wave 3: capital-intensive industries (Years 18-25)

By Year 18, the fund has cash flow from essentials, labor-intensive manufacturing, and healthcare services. It has operational experience running large companies. It has demonstrated political legitimacy by surviving a decade of operations across many countries. Now it can tackle the hardest, most expensive, longest-horizon industries.

Wave 3 covers:

- Steel, aluminum, and base metals production
- Cement and construction materials at scale
- Energy infrastructure (refineries, pipelines where politically feasible, renewable installations)
- Heavy machinery and industrial equipment
- Advanced pharmaceutical research (not just generic manufacturing — the harder, more expensive work of drug discovery)
- Logistics and shipping infrastructure
- Telecoms infrastructure in cooperative jurisdictions

These are industries where investment horizons run 15-30 years, where private equity often does poorly because it can't be patient enough, and where the fund's structural willingness to wait pays off. Returns are lower than in software or finance, but they're stable for decades — which is what the fund's members want.

The margin trajectory continues here. Capital-intensive industries often run higher margins than labor-intensive ones — 10-15% net margins in steel and cement, 15-25% in advanced pharma. The reinvestment stream from acquisitions in this wave is substantial and funds entry into the harder Wave 4 industries.

## Wave 4: harder industries (Years 25-35+)

Wave 4 covers industries where the fund's structural advantages apply most slowly and incompletely:

- Financial services (consumer banking, insurance, investment management beyond fund operations themselves)
- Advanced technology hardware (semiconductors, advanced batteries, EVs)
- Software and platform services
- Commercial aerospace
- Luxury goods, jewelry, and high-end design

Each of these has a different reason for being late, and the strategy in each is different.

*Financial services beyond what Wave 1 covers.* By Wave 4, the fund's digital banks have grown to compete with major retail banks in many countries. The fund's insurance arm grows out of the healthcare coverage offering. The fund's investment management arm runs the fund's own treasury operations and could plausibly extend to consumer wealth management products. The fund probably becomes one of the world's largest financial institutions by the end of Wave 4, but the customer-facing financial services compete with established private banks that have decades of relationship history and brand trust.

*Advanced technology hardware.* The fund probably doesn't displace TSMC, Samsung, or Intel in semiconductors. The R&D investment required is enormous, the talent concentration is extreme, and the existing competitors have decades of accumulated expertise. The fund's strategy here is to acquire smaller specialized players and operate them at lower margins than the current market accepts — possibly contributing to a longer-term price reduction in advanced tech without trying to "win" the market outright. Modest share (5-10%) is realistic.

*Software and platform services.* The fund's strategy in software is distinct from in other industries. Existing open-source alternatives are already viable competitors to most major proprietary platforms — Linux against Windows, Postgres against Oracle, LibreOffice against Microsoft 365. The fund's role here is less about acquiring proprietary giants and more about acquiring or building major distributions of open-source software, then offering them as fund-owned enterprise products with support, training, and integration. The fund effectively becomes the enterprise distribution channel for the open-source ecosystem. The European Union's recent moves toward Linux-based government computing (driven by cost, security, and concerns about US tech dependence) suggest this market is increasingly receptive.

*Commercial aerospace.* The aviation industry is transportation infrastructure — it moves people and goods, connects families across borders, makes possible forms of human exchange that would otherwise be impossible. Currently the industry operates with significant pricing power: airlines charge what markets will bear rather than what services cost, and aircraft manufacturers (Boeing, Airbus) operate behind enormous barriers to entry that protect their margins. A fund-owned commercial aerospace presence — building aircraft, operating an airline, or both — extends the fund's mission of making essential services affordable to ordinary people. The political case is straightforward and the structural advantages apply in the usual ways.

*Luxury goods.* This is the most interesting case in Wave 4.

The original framing might be that the fund leaves luxury to private capital — no one needs a \$200,000 watch, the fund's mission is essentials, luxury markets depend on exclusivity that fund ownership might undermine. But there's a stronger argument for fund participation in luxury, and it starts with the talent.

Luxury goods are, fundamentally, the work of exceptional designers and craftsmen. A handbag from a top-tier house isn't just leather and stitching — it's the accumulated work of generations of master designers and artisans. A high-end watch represents the work of master watchmakers. Luxury jewelry depends on master gemologists and designers. These are talented people who produce extraordinary work, and the current model rewards them through employment at brands whose value rests on private ownership and exclusivity.

The fund's entry into luxury changes the talent model. The fund creates a structure where exceptional designers and craftsmen can join through the fund's innovation and royalties pathway. A designer creates a piece for a fund-owned luxury house and receives royalties on its sales for as long as the design generates revenue — much as a fund-affiliated scientist receives royalties on a drug they helped develop. The talent is rewarded directly through the value they create rather than through employment at a private brand. The fund's role in luxury is, first and foremost, to support exceptional creators and provide them an alternative pathway to participating in the value they generate.

This produces a luxury market that does two things. First, it offers a path for top creative talent to participate in the fund as creators rather than just as members — capital, labor, and ideas, all integrated. Second, it captures the spending of people who continue to buy luxury goods during the long transition era. Those purchases flow into fund profits and back to all members as dividends, rather than into the accounts of luxury holding companies' private shareholders.

There's a longer-horizon argument as well. As the fund's effects on essentials, healthcare, manufacturing, and finance compound over decades, the extreme concentration of wealth that supports the current luxury market should gradually moderate. With fewer billionaires bidding up \$200,000 watches, the luxury market itself becomes smaller and less extreme. The fund's presence accelerates this — fund-owned luxury at fair margins competes against private luxury at extractive margins, and the price umbrella that lets private luxury charge what it charges erodes over time. Eventually the category looks less like the current ostentation market and more like the high-craft market it was historically: real designers making real masterpieces at prices that reflect the work, not the markup.

The fund enters luxury in Wave 4 because it's lower priority than essentials, healthcare, and basic manufacturing. But it enters. The fund is not above this market.

## What the fund deliberately doesn't enter, ever

Some categories of economic activity remain outside the fund's scope by design.

*Raw materials and resource extraction.* As Part 1 established, oil, mining, and other resource industries belong to the citizens of the countries where those resources sit. The fund doesn't own national oil companies, mining giants, or extractive enterprises. It purchases raw materials at fair prices from citizen-owned national resource companies that *should* exist in resource-rich countries. The fund's role is downstream of extraction, not at the well or the mine.

*Defense and weapons manufacturing.* The fund's purpose is to make economic transactions fair, distribute power more evenly, and reduce the structural conditions that produce wars. Defense contracting profits from exactly the opposite — every conflict, every arms race, every weapons sale is revenue. A fund-owned defense company would have a built-in financial interest in geopolitical instability that would corrupt the fund's purpose. There is also an arithmetic problem with universal ownership: a fund member in any country at war with any other country would, in some abstract sense, own a share of weapons being used against citizens of a country whose members are also co-owners. The math of universal ownership doesn't work in defense. The fund stays out — not because defense contracting can't be profitable, but because profit from conflict is incompatible with what the fund exists to do.

*Speculative and short-horizon financial instruments.* The fund doesn't operate hedge funds, day-trading platforms, cryptocurrency exchanges, derivatives markets, or sports gambling. Its capital is patient and long-horizon. It would be a bad operator in markets that reward speed and risk-taking, and its members aren't trying to bet — they're trying to own.

*The stock exchange itself.* The fund operates outside the stock exchange. Its acquired companies are taken private. The fund's own membership shares are not traded; they exist on the fund's own books and pay dividends to identified members. The fund's capital structure isn't subject to short-selling, derivatives, market manipulation, or the quarterly earnings pressures that bend public companies away from their stated missions. This is a deliberate choice, not an oversight.

*Heavily-regulated state monopolies.* National defense (already excluded above), central banking, currency issuance, judicial systems, electoral systems. The fund doesn't compete with government where government legitimately operates. These remain state-run.

*Personal services and inherently local industries.* The fund doesn't own your barbershop, your local restaurant, or your neighborhood plumber. These industries are inherently local, low-capital, and inherently entrepreneurial. They flourish when consumers have purchasing power — which the fund's dividends and manufacturing redistribution help create. The fund's role here is indirect: it strengthens local economies, which lets local services thrive on their own terms.

## The environmental dimension running through everything

Across all four waves of fund acquisitions, an environmental responsibility runs through the fund's choices. The current global economy treats environmental damage as an externality — a cost imposed on the future, on people who didn't consent, on ecosystems that can't bill for the damage done to them. The fund's broader mission of redistributing value fairly cannot ignore this. A fund that captures the value currently extracted by private corporations but leaves the environmental damage uncaptured is just redistributing among humans while still extracting from the planet.

Two specific commitments follow from this. First, the fund prioritizes acquisitions and new ventures that move away from environmentally destructive practices when realistic alternatives exist. Fund-owned cement research focuses on lower-carbon formulations. Fund-owned steel investments prioritize electric-arc and hydrogen-reduction technologies over coal-based blast furnaces where transition is feasible. Fund-owned vehicle manufacturing focuses on electric and hybrid platforms over pure combustion. The fund accepts somewhat lower short-term returns on these choices because the alternative — continuing to profit from environmentally destructive industries while ignoring the costs — would contradict the fund's purpose.

Second, the fund invests heavily in alternative energy research and development. This is the area where the fund's patient-capital advantage matters most. Solar, wind, geothermal, tidal, advanced nuclear, hydrogen production, energy storage — these technologies need sustained investment over decades to mature. Private capital systematically underinvests because the payback horizons don't match quarterly reporting cycles. The fund's structure makes it the natural patient investor in clean energy infrastructure. The fund's commercial aerospace presence in Wave 4 connects directly to this: fund-owned aircraft will, over time, run on fund-invested sustainable aviation fuels and eventually hydrogen and electric propulsion. The fund's vehicle parts manufacturing in Wave 2 connects to fund-owned battery technology and grid infrastructure. The energy transition the world needs is, structurally, an investment problem the fund is well-positioned to solve.

What the fund does not do is preach about environmental responsibility while continuing to operate destructively. The commitment is operational — it shapes what gets acquired, what gets researched, what gets built. The fund's accumulated reinvestment from the margin trajectory described above is a substantial pool of patient capital, and a meaningful share of it goes into the technologies that make a sustainable economy possible. Members can verify this in fund operations because the relevant investments are disclosed and auditable, like everything else the fund does.

## What the fund owns by Year 30

Putting it together: by roughly Year 30 of operation, in the scenario where the fund succeeds at most of what it attempts:

- Substantial share (20-30%) of essential goods industries: basic pharma, staple food, basic utilities, basic consumer goods
- Meaningful share (15-25%) of labor-intensive manufacturing: textiles, furniture, electronics assembly, vehicle parts
- Significant share (15-20%) of healthcare services in countries where regulatory environment permits
- Significant share (15-20%) of capital-intensive industries: steel, cement, heavy machinery, advanced pharma, infrastructure
- Meaningful share (10-20%) of consumer financial services: digital banking, insurance, basic credit, card network
- Modest share (5-15%) of harder industries: software (mostly through open-source enterprise distribution), advanced tech hardware, commercial aerospace, luxury goods
- Effectively zero share of resource extraction (handled by citizen-owned national companies), defense, speculative finance, state monopolies, and local personal services

In total, the fund might own something like 12-18% of global productive economic activity by Year 30. That number is meaningful but not threatening — and the framing matters.

The 12-18% figure isn't a ceiling the fund is aspiring to reach. It's the strategic position the fund deliberately occupies. Owning 12-18% is enough to set norms that the remaining 82-88% has to respond to. Fund-owned essentials companies establish wage floors and price ceilings that private competitors have to consider. Fund-owned manufacturing pulls supply chains toward regional sourcing that private competitors then have to match for political reasons. Fund-owned healthcare and banking offer alternatives to extractive private services that force the private versions to compete on more favorable terms.

This is the difference between competitive influence and monopolistic dominance. The fund is the largest single economic entity in human history at 12-18% of global production — and chooses to stop there because larger would trigger the kind of political response that smaller doesn't. Oligarchs and hostile governments are less likely to organize against a fund that operates at this scale than against one that's clearly trying to take over everything. The fund's strategic patience compounds: by appearing non-threatening, the fund extends the political runway during which it can grow without serious resistance.

## The honest version of this trajectory

I want to be clear about what this article is and isn't.

It is: a plausible sequence based on the fund's principles, the structural advantages it has in different industries, and the capital it can accumulate over time. The pacing is grounded in Part 1's adoption math and the realistic operational complexity of acquiring and operating large companies.

It isn't: a fixed plan. The actual sequence will be voted on by the fund's members, will adapt to what fund leadership can execute well, and will be shaped by which countries are willing to host fund operations early. Some industries on this list may be moved earlier or later. Some might be skipped. Others might be added as new industries emerge over the next thirty years that don't exist today.

What's most likely to change in practice: the pacing of healthcare and finance. Both are politically sensitive and regulatory-heavy. They may move faster in some countries than the wave structure suggests, slower in others. The wave structure describes a default sequence; the actual rollout will be country-by-country and industry-by-industry, voted on by members at each stage.

The point isn't to predict 2055 precisely. It's to show that "what the fund owns" has a coherent shape, follows principles, and produces a recognizable institution at the end. The fund isn't trying to own everything. It's trying to own the right things, in the right order, well enough to change how the global economy works.
