# How the Money Flows: Contributions, Currency, Dividends, Overhead

The previous articles established what the fund is, what it owns, and how it competes. This one walks through what happens to your \$1 a month — from the moment it leaves your account to the moment a dividend arrives back. There are five distinct flows: contribution, aggregation, currency handling, deployment, and dividend distribution, plus a sixth question about overhead — what it costs to run the institution itself.

The mechanics matter because trust in any financial institution depends on understanding how it actually works. Banks that obscure their operations lose customer trust. Funds that hide their fees lose investor trust. The fund's strategy is the opposite: every flow described here is intended to be auditable, comparable across countries, and visible to members at any time they choose to look.

## 1. Contributing your dollar

A member's \$1 monthly contribution can enter the fund through several paths, depending on the country and the member's existing financial relationships.

For members with a bank account, the contribution is a recurring transfer of the local-currency equivalent of \$1 to the fund's account in their country. Most members will set this up once and forget about it — the contribution runs automatically each month, comparable to a streaming service subscription. In practice the amount is calculated based on the prior month's currency reference rate, so a member in Brazil contributes the *real* equivalent of \$1, a member in India contributes the *rupee* equivalent, and so on. Small fluctuations in exchange rates produce small fluctuations in the local-currency amount, but the underlying commitment remains constant.

For members with mobile money but no traditional bank account, the contribution flows through the local mobile money operator (M-Pesa in Kenya, GCash in the Philippines, bKash in Bangladesh, Mercado Pago in much of Latin America, and similar systems elsewhere). The fund integrates with established mobile money networks rather than trying to replace them. A member's phone contains their entire participation in the fund: contribution, balance, transaction history, dividend deposits.

For members with neither bank accounts nor mobile money — a population of roughly 1.4 billion adults globally, concentrated in rural areas of low-income countries — the fund operates through participating retailers and agents. A member walks into a participating shop, pays the local equivalent of \$1 in cash, presents identification (or their member identifier if already enrolled), and the contribution is recorded. This is how Western Union and the major mobile money networks handle cash-based customers; the fund uses the same model. The retailer earns a small fee for handling the transaction. Over time, as the fund's own digital banks expand into these areas, the retailer model gives way to direct mobile banking — but the cash path remains available indefinitely for people who prefer it or have no other option.

For new members joining the fund for the first time, the enrollment process establishes their identity, their country of residence, their preferred contribution method, and their preferred dividend destination. A child enrolled at birth receives an account in their name with contributions made by relatives or by the donation fund described in Part 1. Identification requirements vary by country and follow local know-your-customer regulations; the fund cannot operate outside national financial law and doesn't try to.

The friction in joining is intentionally low. The fund's growth depends on enrollment being easy enough that people who broadly agree with the mission can sign up without overcoming significant procedural barriers.

## 2. Aggregating contributions: the local-first principle

Once a contribution is made, the money stays in the country where it was contributed, denominated in the local currency. This is a deliberate strategic choice with three operational benefits.

First, money that stays where it is contributed cannot be intercepted by financial sanctions. If a hostile foreign government attempts to freeze the fund's assets in dollar-denominated accounts, the fund's operations in that government's jurisdiction are affected — but the fund's holdings in dozens of other countries are not. By contrast, a fund that routed all contributions through a central dollar-denominated treasury would be entirely vulnerable to any government with influence over the dollar system.

Second, no daily currency conversion happens. The contributions remain in local currency until the fund decides to deploy them. Daily currency conversion would expose the fund to constant exchange-rate volatility and would generate transaction costs that erode the contribution before it can be invested. The fund moves currency across borders only when an acquisition or operation requires it, not as a routine administrative matter.

Third, the local-first structure aligns fund operations with the economies the fund operates in. Contributions made in Brazilian reais fund Brazilian operations until Brazilian operations are funded; only the surplus moves to other markets. This produces a natural balance between contribution-generating regions and capital-deploying regions, and it means each country's contributions visibly support that country's economic development first.

In practice, contributions flow into the fund's digital bank in each country (the financial infrastructure described in Article 2-3). The bank holds the contributions, deploys them locally where appropriate (lending to fund-aligned local businesses, investing in local fund acquisitions, supporting the fund's local operations), and transfers any surplus to the fund's broader treasury operations only when deployment opportunities elsewhere exceed local needs.

## 3. Currency handling: the basket reference unit

The fund's accounting requires a common reference point because operations span more than 80 countries with different currencies, inflation rates, and economic conditions. The fund cannot report results in 80 different currencies simultaneously — it needs one unit of account for internal planning, financial reporting, and the dividend calculation that ensures equality across members.

The fund's reference unit is a weighted basket of major world currencies. The composition includes the most widely used reserve currencies and the currencies of the largest economies, weighted by a combination of trade volume, GDP, and member population in the corresponding currency zones. The current basket includes the US dollar, the euro, the Chinese yuan, the Japanese yen, the British pound, the Indian rupee, the Brazilian real, the Russian ruble, the South African rand, and a rotating set of additional currencies representing other major economies. The exact composition is reviewed by the fund's governance every five years and adjusted as global economic weights shift.

This basket approach has several advantages. The fund's value isn't tied to the fluctuations of any single currency, which protects members in any one country from being disadvantaged by their currency's relative movements. The basket naturally rebalances as economies grow and shrink, so the fund's reference unit tracks the actual global economy rather than tracking the economy of one dominant country. And the basket avoids placing the fund inside any single country's financial control regime; the fund doesn't operate "on dollars" or "on yuan" but on a weighted measure that no single government can manipulate.

When the fund reports its asset base, profit, dividend pool, or any other aggregate financial figure, it does so in basket reference units. When the fund communicates with individual members about their dividend, it converts the basket-denominated dividend into the member's local currency at the time of distribution. A member in Vietnam receives their dividend in dong; a member in Nigeria receives theirs in naira; a member in Germany receives theirs in euros. The conversion is transparent and audited, and the conversion rate used is published with each dividend distribution.

Members can choose, if they prefer, to receive their dividend in basket reference units stored in their fund account rather than converted to local currency. This option matters for members in countries with very high inflation or very volatile currencies — they can preserve the value of their dividend in basket terms rather than watching local-currency dividends lose purchasing power between distributions.

## 4. Deploying capital into acquisitions and operations

Aggregated contributions accumulate in the fund's country-level banks. When deployment is needed — for an acquisition, for a new fund operation, for supporting fund-aligned businesses — the deployment process follows a defined sequence.

For acquisitions, the process begins with identification. The fund's acquisition team, operating under the governance described in Part 1, identifies candidate companies in the sectors the fund is targeting. The team conducts due diligence on the company's financials, operations, supply chains, labor practices, environmental record, and strategic fit. The team prepares an acquisition proposal that includes the purchase price, the expected return profile, the operational changes the fund would make after acquisition (the margin trajectory, the wage and supplier improvements, the integration with other fund operations), and the financing plan (which contributions from which countries fund which portions of the purchase).

The acquisition proposal goes to the Citizen Chamber and the Country Chamber for approval. Smaller acquisitions (below a defined threshold) may be approved by the acquisition committee directly with chamber notification; larger acquisitions require formal chamber votes. The bicameral structure ensures that both global one-person-one-vote and per-country one-vote perspectives bear on the decision.

If approved, the fund deploys the necessary capital. For a major acquisition, this typically involves consolidating funds from multiple country-level accounts into a coordinated treasury operation, converting currencies as needed to match the seller's denomination, and executing the purchase through standard corporate acquisition processes. The acquired company becomes a fund-owned subsidiary, operating under fund governance and aligned with fund principles.

For existing shareholders of acquired companies, the fund offers fair market value at the time of acquisition. Where shareholders are pension funds, retirement accounts, or other long-term holders of capital, the fund offers the option of converting their shares into other forms of fund-aligned investment (the fund's bonds, fund-issued investment certificates representing claims on specific fund operations, or cash). The transition is designed to be acceptable to existing capital structures rather than disruptive — the fund acquires by purchasing, not by expropriating.

For acquired companies' employees, the fund commits to retention through the integration period and to maintaining or improving compensation levels. Executive teams of acquired companies are evaluated individually; some are retained, some are replaced with fund-aligned leadership over time. The pace of change varies by acquisition and is governed by member-elected boards.

For acquired companies' supply chains, the fund renegotiates terms to better align with fund principles — fair payment, longer-term contracts, support for fund-aligned suppliers where possible. These changes happen gradually rather than immediately, since abrupt changes would disrupt operations.

For deployment into fund-aligned local businesses (the zero-cost capital arrangement described in Article 2-3), the fund's bank in each country evaluates applications, performs due diligence, and provides capital to qualifying businesses. These deployments are smaller individually but numerous, and they happen through standard banking processes rather than through formal chamber votes.

## 5. From operations to dividends

Fund-owned companies generate profits through their normal commercial operations. Those profits flow to the fund through the standard corporate financial structures — quarterly distributions, annual dividends to the fund as the parent entity, and reinvested earnings. The fund consolidates these flows from all its acquired companies and operations into a single profit pool at each accounting period.

The dividend calculation works as follows. The fund's total annual profit, denominated in basket reference units, is divided into three components.

The first component covers the fund's operating costs (overhead) for the year. This is a defined budget approved by the chambers, not a residual.

The second component is reinvestment for expansion. For companies acquired at margins above 10%, the difference between their actual margin and the 10% target is automatically reinvested rather than distributed, as described in Article 2-3 — this is invisible to members because it never enters the dividend pool. Beyond this margin-trajectory reinvestment, any additional reinvestment from the dividend pool requires a member vote. By default, no member dividend money is reinvested without consent.

The third component is the dividend pool — what remains after overhead and approved reinvestment. This pool is divided equally among all fund members worldwide. This is the equality principle made operational.

The per-member dividend is then distributed. Members who chose local-currency distribution receive their dividend in their local currency at the published conversion rate. Members who chose basket-unit distribution have their dividend credited to their fund account in basket units. Members with mobile money or digital bank accounts receive direct deposits. Members in the cash retailer system can withdraw their dividend through participating retailers.

In the early years of the fund, dividends are distributed annually. As the fund matures and dividend amounts grow, the chambers may approve more frequent distributions — semi-annually, quarterly, or even monthly. The administrative cost of more frequent distributions has to be weighed against the benefit to members of receiving income more regularly.

Tax treatment of dividends varies by country. The fund does not have the authority to override national tax law, and members are responsible for declaring and paying tax on dividends in their country of residence. The fund publishes for each country a comparison of how dividend income is treated under that country's tax code, both to help members understand their obligations and to make visible the differences between tax regimes. In some countries, cooperative-style dividends are tax-favored; in others, they're taxed as ordinary income; in others, they're treated as capital gains. The fund itself takes no position on what the right tax policy is — that's a matter for each country's citizens and their democratic processes. The fund simply complies with local law and provides the information members need to comply themselves.

## 6. Overhead: what it costs to run the fund

The fund's overhead covers everything required to operate the institution: technology infrastructure (member systems, banking platforms, payment networks, financial reporting); compliance and regulatory affairs across more than 80 jurisdictions; legal services for acquisitions, contracts, and disputes; audit by independent external auditors; customer service in dozens of languages; governance operations (chamber elections, voting infrastructure, deliberative processes); the AI mediation system from Part 1's three-stage cascade; treasury operations; and the staff salaries that make all of this possible.

The overhead is significant in absolute terms and varies dramatically in percentage terms over the fund's life.

In year 1, with perhaps 100 million members contributing roughly \$1.2 billion annually, the fund needs to build essentially all of its infrastructure from scratch. Overhead in year 1 could reasonably consume 30-40% of contributions — a large percentage of a relatively small base. The fund is in startup mode; the overhead is paying for things that didn't exist before.

By year 5, with membership perhaps 1.5 billion and contributions around \$18 billion annually, the infrastructure is largely built. Marginal cost per additional member is small. Overhead might decline to 15-20% as the base scales faster than the costs.

By year 15, with membership approaching universal coverage and contributions plus profits generating a much larger total pool, overhead might fall to 8-12%. The fund is mature; its operations are routinized; its scale economies are working.

In the long-term steady state, the target overhead is roughly 5-8%. This is comparable to well-run cooperative banks and substantially better than most private financial institutions. Most private mutual funds run overhead in the range of 0.5-2% but only on managed assets, not on contributions; on a contribution-flow basis, comparable financial institutions typically run higher.

The overhead trajectory matters for two reasons. First, it explains why dividends in the early years are modest: in those years, the fund is investing in itself, building the infrastructure that makes the long-term dividend possible. Second, it sets a transparency standard. The chambers approve the overhead budget annually. The actual overhead is reported quarterly. Any deviation from the approved budget requires explanation. Members can see, in real time, what portion of their contribution is being spent on operations versus invested or distributed.

If overhead were to balloon — as has happened with many large institutions over time — members would see it and could vote to constrain it. The fund's governance includes specific provisions making overhead growth a high-visibility matter: the chambers cannot quietly let overhead drift upward without explicit decisions, and constitutional limits cap overhead at a defined percentage of revenue that requires extraordinary majorities to exceed. The fund is structurally constrained from becoming bloated.

## 7. The donation fund mechanics

A specific operational detail worth covering: how donations actually reach the people they support.

The donation fund operates as a separate accounting structure within the broader fund. Donors contribute through normal fund banking channels — bank transfer, mobile money, or directly through the fund's website — and indicate that their contribution is to the donation fund rather than to their own membership. The donation fund pools these contributions globally.

The donation fund then covers the \$1 monthly contribution for members who have indicated they cannot afford it. Eligibility is determined through a combination of self-declaration and country-specific criteria (since "cannot afford \$1" means different things in different economies). The donated contributions flow into the regular contribution system, generating regular dividends for the supported members.

As supported members' dividends grow — typically over 5 to 10 years, depending on the fund's overall growth and the member's country — the dividends eventually exceed the \$1 monthly contribution amount. At that point, the donation fund stops paying that member's contribution; the member's own dividends fund their continued membership. The donation arm's role for that member ends.

This is the self-extinguishing property described in Article 2-2. A donor's \$12 a year contribution to the donation fund eventually produces a self-sustaining fund member who no longer requires support. The donor's role ends; the recipient becomes a permanent co-owner.

The donation fund's accounting is fully transparent: how much was contributed, how many members were supported, how many graduated to self-sufficiency, and how the donation pool was deployed. Donors can verify their contributions' impact directly.

## 8. What members can verify

The fund's operations are designed to be auditable by members at multiple levels.

Annually, the fund publishes a comprehensive financial report including total contributions received (by country and currency), total assets held, profit by operating segment, dividend pool and distribution, overhead breakdown, and reinvestment by category. This report is audited by an entity selected by the chambers. In the early years and likely for decades, the auditor is a major external audit firm with an international reputation to protect — its independence from the fund is what makes the audit credible. As the fund matures, the chambers may choose to develop fund-owned audit capability, but the choice between external and internal audit always remains a chamber decision, voted on periodically, with safeguards against collusion built into the selection process. Specifically, no auditor (external or internal) can serve more than a defined number of consecutive years without rotation; the auditor reports directly to the chambers rather than to fund management; and the chambers retain the authority to commission supplemental audits by other entities at any time if concerns arise. The principle is that audit independence is structural, not just procedural — the auditor's accountability runs to the members, through the chambers, not to the management of the institution being audited.

Quarterly, the fund publishes interim financials showing major changes since the prior report. Members can see acquisitions completed, dividends distributed, overhead trends, and any significant operational developments.

Continuously, members have access to their own account information: contributions made, dividends received, balance in basket units if they chose that option, status of any donation-fund support. The member portal shows all of this in the member's preferred language.

For deeper engagement, members can review the chamber meeting minutes, voting records, and proposal documents. The deliberative processes that produce fund decisions are public. Members can submit proposals, comment on pending decisions, and observe how their representatives vote.

An AI system designed for member engagement makes this depth of involvement practical for ordinary members who don't have time to read every meeting transcript. Members can ask the AI questions about fund operations in plain language — "how much did the fund spend on overhead in my country last year," "what acquisitions are pending chamber approval," "how did my country's representatives vote on the last energy infrastructure proposal," "what's the dividend trajectory in countries similar to mine" — and receive answers grounded in the fund's public documents. The same AI helps members draft proposals, organize comments, and connect with other members interested in similar issues. The AI doesn't make decisions for members; it helps members participate more meaningfully in decisions that would otherwise require hours of document review. Like the rest of the fund's AI infrastructure, this engagement system is open-source, with publicly auditable training data, so members can verify that the AI is providing accurate information rather than steering them toward fund management's preferred conclusions.

The fund publishes, for each country it operates in, a comparison of how key financial flows are handled in that country versus others. This includes tax treatment of dividends, regulatory requirements on the fund's bank, restrictions on capital movement, and any country-specific operational variations. The comparison is informational; it doesn't recommend that countries change their policies. But it makes visible to citizens of each country how their country's approach compares to others, which lets them advocate within their own democratic processes for whatever changes they prefer.

The transparency standard is high because the fund's legitimacy depends on it. Most financial institutions disclose what they're legally required to disclose and no more. The fund discloses comprehensively because its members are its owners, and owners have a right to know what their institution is doing.

## Why this matters

The mechanics described in this article aren't glamorous, but they're foundational. A fund that handles billions of small contributions, deploys hundreds of billions of dollars in acquisitions, manages dozens of currencies, complies with the financial law of more than 80 countries, and distributes dividends back to billions of people across the planet is one of the most operationally complex institutions ever attempted.

The complexity is justified by the outcome. When the operations work, a person in rural Vietnam, a person in suburban Indiana, a person in a coastal village in Senegal, and a person in central London all participate in the same institution on equal terms, receive equal dividends, and exercise equal votes. The mechanics make the equality real. Without working operations, the equality is just an aspiration.

The fund's growth over decades depends on these flows continuing to work, becoming more efficient over time, and remaining transparent throughout. Every operational improvement — a more efficient mobile money integration, a lower-cost cross-border settlement system, a more user-friendly dividend distribution interface — translates into better outcomes for members. The fund's operations team is one of the institution's most important groups, and the operations themselves are one of the fund's most important products.
