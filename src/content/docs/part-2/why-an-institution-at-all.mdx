# Why an Institution at All

The most beautiful idea in the world doesn't change anything until someone builds the machinery to run it.

History is littered with movements that had the right diagnosis and the wrong follow-through. The original cooperative movement of the 1840s correctly identified that workers were being exploited by capital — and produced thousands of small co-ops that mostly failed within a generation because they had no institutional structure to compete with corporations at scale. The League of Nations correctly identified that nations needed a forum to prevent war — and collapsed within twenty years because it had no enforcement mechanism. The Universal Declaration of Human Rights correctly identified what every human is entitled to — and has been violated routinely for 77 years because no institution exists to actually deliver those rights.

The lesson is uncomfortable but clear: **a movement is not a system**. A diagnosis is not a cure. A demand is not a structure. If we want the world Part 1 of this series proposed — power distributed to every human being, competition between citizen-owned and privately-owned companies, dividends that turn citizens into shareholders of the global economy — then we have to build something that can actually do those things. The \$1 a month is the easy part. The institution that holds it, invests it, governs it, and returns dividends from it is the hard part.

Part 2 is about that institution. Eight articles, each addressing one question a reader has after finishing Part 1.

## Why this matters more than it might seem

Most readers will assume the fund described in Part 1 is roughly like things they already know about — a sovereign wealth fund, or a giant index fund, or a hedge fund, or maybe just a charity. It isn't any of those. The differences matter, because they explain why this fund can do things existing institutions can't.

A sovereign wealth fund (Norway's Government Pension Fund, the Abu Dhabi Investment Authority, Singapore's GIC) is owned by a government, serves that country's strategic objectives, and pays out to that country's citizens through state services. It is, in effect, one country's extension of its political power into global capital markets. The fund is owned by individual humans regardless of citizenship, serves the interests of all participants equally, and pays dividends directly to each member. It has no flag.

An index fund (Vanguard's S&P 500 fund, BlackRock's iShares) is owned by its individual investors but votes its shares passively — it doesn't try to change what the companies it owns actually do. Vanguard owns 8% of every major US company on average; if it wanted to, it could probably end fossil fuel investment in a board vote. It doesn't, because that's not what its investors signed up for. The fund is the opposite — its entire purpose is to actively shape what its companies do: where they manufacture, what they charge, how they treat workers, what margins they accept.

A hedge fund is owned by wealthy investors and operates to maximize their returns; the fund is owned by everyone equally and operates to maximize collective welfare. A charity collects donations and gives them away; the fund collects contributions and invests them to generate sustainable returns for its members.

What the fund is closest to — and this is worth saying clearly — is **a global, cross-border version of what Mondragón has done in Spain for sixty-five years**. Mondragón is a federation of cooperatives founded in 1956 in the Basque Country. It now employs over 70,000 people across 95 cooperatives, generates over €11 billion in annual revenue, and operates in finance, retail, manufacturing, and technology. Workers own it. Workers govern it through one-person-one-vote in their cooperative and through representatives in the federal Congress. It competes with conventional corporations across multiple industries. It has survived recessions, expansions, and the slow normalization of "neoliberal" capitalism around it. It is, simply, a working example of what citizen-owned competitive enterprise looks like at scale.

The fund extends Mondragón's principle from a regional federation of workers to a global federation of citizens. From "the people who work here own this company" to "every human being on earth owns one share of the global productive system." The principles are the same. The scale is unprecedented.

## Why does Part 2 even exist?

Because the philosophical case in Part 1 doesn't survive contact with a determined skeptic without an institutional answer.

A skeptic reading Part 1 will ask: "If everyone really owned the world's largest companies through this fund, what stops a small group inside the fund from taking it over? What stops the fund managers from becoming the new oligarchs? What stops one government from controlling it? What stops the fund from becoming a slow, bureaucratic mess like the UN? How does the fund actually compete with companies that have decades of head start? How do you collect \$1 from someone in rural Bangladesh who has no bank account? What happens if a country bans the fund from operating? Who decides which companies to buy?"

These are not bad-faith questions. They are the questions any thoughtful person would ask before participating in something this ambitious. Part 1 doesn't answer them in detail because Part 1's job was to make the case for *why this should exist*. Part 2's job is to answer them — to show that the institution can actually be built, governed, defended against corruption, and operated at scale, and that the answers are real engineering choices rather than wishful thinking.

If we can't answer those questions credibly, the fund stays an interesting idea in a Substack post. If we can answer them — and I believe we can, though imperfectly and with real trade-offs — then it becomes something that could actually happen.

## What's coming in Part 2

The eight articles that follow address, in order:

- *What the Fund Is and What It Isn't* — direct comparison to existing institutions
- *What the Fund Owns and How That Grows* — the acquisition trajectory across the 30 years
- *How the Fund Competes (and Why It Wins Some Markets, Not All)* — honest engagement with the competitive question
- *How the Money Flows* — contributions, currency, dividends, overhead, mechanics
- *How the Fund Gets Built* — the bootstrapping problem and the founders question
- *What Stops the Fund from Becoming What It Replaces* — operational anti-corruption
- *What the First Decade Could Look Like* — concrete trajectory in years 1-10

Each article is short enough to read in one sitting. You can read them in order or jump to whichever question matters most to you. You can disagree with any of them and still find the rest useful.

What I won't do is pretend this is easy or that all the questions have clean answers. Building an institution that owns a meaningful share of the global economy and is governed by 8 billion people is the hardest design problem in modern political economy. We don't have a working template. We have precedents (Mondragón, Norwegian SWF, EU institutions, Wikipedia, cooperative banks) that solve pieces of the problem. We have to assemble them into something new.
