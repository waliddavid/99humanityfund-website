# How the Fund Competes

Most people want simple things. Enough income to feed their family. Goods and services they can afford. The chance to build something meaningful with their lives. Safety for their children. Peace with people in other countries who want exactly the same things.

Most people don't want wars. Most people don't want their tax money funding weapons that destroy other people's homes. Most people don't want their government installing or removing leaders in other countries based on which arrangement produces the best deal for corporations they don't own and never will. Most people don't want to send their children to die for objectives no one fully explains to them. Most people understand, even when no one says it out loud, that what they share with strangers across borders — the basic human wish to live and to provide and to grow old surrounded by people they love — is more real than the differences their governments insist on emphasizing.

The polling confirms this isn't a sentimental view, it's a documented majority position across the world. A 2024 Pew Research Center survey of 36 countries with 41,503 adult respondents found that 84% of people see the gap between rich and poor as at least a moderately big problem in their country, and 60% identified rich people having too much political influence as a major cause. A World Values Survey of 66 countries found that nearly half of all respondents say "the rich often buy elections" in their country. A 2026 survey by Democracy Without Borders covering 101 countries representing 90% of the world's population found that 40% of respondents support creating a citizen-elected world parliament — with another 33% neutral — because confidence in the current international system is collapsing. In 85 of the 101 countries, more respondents support the idea than oppose it.

A Gallup International poll across 45 countries representing approximately half of the global population found something even more telling about war specifically: willingness to fight for one's country has declined from 61% in 2014 to roughly half today. The decline is concentrated in exactly the countries whose governments most actively push pro-war narratives — the European Union (32% willing to fight), the United States (41%), and Canada (34%) — compared to populations in West Asia (77%), the broader Middle East (73%), and India (76%). The president of Gallup International put it directly: "The elites in the West might be inclined to solve problems by fighting or supporting a war, but Western citizens are twice less inclined to bear arms compared to the citizens of the rest of the world."

And yet wars keep happening. Governments keep getting overthrown. Resources keep being extracted from poor countries on terms that benefit a small number of shareholders in rich countries. Defense industry stocks keep rising when conflicts begin and falling when they end.

How does this keep happening when most people don't want it?

## The pattern since World War II

The honest answer to that question is uncomfortable but well-documented. Since World War II, US foreign policy has consistently included interventions to secure resource access, install or remove governments, and prioritize the interests of major US corporations in foreign markets. This is not a fringe interpretation. The interventions are documented in US government archives, in congressional records, and in mainstream diplomatic history.

A few of the documented examples: In 1953, the US and UK organized a coup in Iran (Operation Ajax) that overthrew the elected government of Mohammad Mosaddegh after he nationalized Iranian oil. In 1954, the US organized a coup in Guatemala that overthrew the elected government of Jacobo Árbenz after his land reform program threatened the interests of the United Fruit Company. In 1973, the US supported the coup that overthrew the elected government of Salvador Allende in Chile, partly motivated by Allende's nationalization of copper mines owned by US corporations. In 2003, the US invaded Iraq under the publicly stated justification of weapons of mass destruction that turned out not to exist; the resulting reconstruction and oil contracts were awarded primarily to US corporations. In 2011, the US-led intervention in Libya overthrew Muammar Gaddafi, whose plans for an African gold-backed currency would have undermined the dollar's role in African trade.

The pattern is consistent. A country resists foreign corporate interests, or attempts to control its own resources, or builds political alliances inconvenient to US economic interests, or threatens the dollar's reserve currency status. Pressure escalates from diplomacy, to sanctions, to covert action, to military intervention. Defense contractors profit at every stage. Energy companies, mining companies, and financial firms position themselves for post-conflict access. The pattern doesn't require a conspiracy. It requires only that politicians need campaign funding, that corporations have the most money to give, and that foreign policy gets shaped by who benefits from particular outcomes.

This is not unique to the United States. Russia, China, the United Kingdom, France, and Israel all conduct foreign policy that serves the economic interests of their politically influential domestic actors. The US case is the most consequential because the US is the largest military and financial power, and because the dollar's role in international trade gives the US unique leverage. But the pattern itself is global.

## Today's wars: what they're actually about

The wars in the current period continue the pattern, though with the specific motivations more visible than at most points in history because of social media, leaks, and the increasing inability of governments to maintain narrative control.

*Ukraine since 2022.* Strategic competition over European energy supply (Russian gas vs. US LNG), control over Ukrainian agricultural exports (Ukraine is one of the world's largest wheat producers), and the broader question of whether NATO would expand to Russia's borders. Defense contractor stocks (Lockheed Martin, RTX, Boeing, Northrop Grumman, General Dynamics) outperformed the S&P 500 by 17.82% in the year following Russia's invasion. Lockheed Martin's stock nearly doubled over the three-year war and the company posted record earnings in 2024.

*Gaza since October 2023.* The military operation has killed over 66,000 Palestinians, wounded nearly 170,000 others, and subjected 2.1 million people to siege and starvation conditions, according to UN sources. Among the documented economic dimensions: the Gaza Marine gas field, discovered in 2000 off Gaza's coast, contains an estimated 1.1 to 1.4 trillion cubic feet of natural gas. Under the 1995 Oslo accords, the offshore area belongs to Palestinian jurisdiction. The field has been blocked from development for over 25 years. UNCTAD has documented that the broader Levant Basin contains an estimated 122 trillion cubic feet of natural gas plus 1.7 billion barrels of recoverable oil across territories that include Palestinian areas. Israel's own Leviathan field, with proven reserves of 600 billion cubic meters, generates approximately \$10 billion annually in export revenue. The European Union signed a memorandum of understanding with Israel and Egypt in June 2022 to import Israeli natural gas. Energy security considerations are visibly entangled with the conflict; whether this is the primary driver, a secondary motivation, or one factor among many is debated, but its presence in the picture is documented and substantial.

*The Israel-Iran war of June 2025.* The conflict produced production shutdowns at Israel's Leviathan and Karish fields, temporarily reducing exports to Egypt. Iran has the world's third-largest proven oil reserves and second-largest natural gas reserves. US-led sanctions since 2018 have aimed to reduce Iran's ability to export these resources, with the State Department documenting an ongoing "maximum pressure" campaign that includes sanctioning intermediaries who help Iran sell oil. The broader regional pattern: financial pressure on Iran weakens its ability to support political and military groups (Hamas, Hezbollah, the Houthis) that have positioned themselves as defenders of Palestinian causes. Iran's economic weakness, in this view, is a strategic objective tied to the broader regional alignment of power.

*Yemen since 2015.* US-supplied Saudi-led coalition strikes have produced one of the worst humanitarian catastrophes of the century. Yemen's strategic importance includes the Bab-el-Mandeb strait, through which approximately 10% of global trade passes. Saudi-Iranian regional competition has used Yemen as a proxy battlefield.

*Syria from 2011.* A multi-sided proxy conflict involving the US, Russia, Iran, Turkey, Israel, and various non-state actors. Syria's pipeline geography (between the Persian Gulf and the Mediterranean) and its role in regional alignments with Iran make it strategically valuable. The Syrian conflict has displaced more than 13 million people.

*Sudan from 2023.* Largely off Western media but documented by humanitarian organizations as among the largest current conflicts. Competition over gold mines, agricultural land, and strategic Red Sea access drives much of the violence. The conflict has displaced over 11 million people.

The pattern across these conflicts is not that resource access is the only motivation — religious, ethnic, political, and historical factors all matter. The pattern is that resource access and corporate economic interests are *always part of the picture*, even when public discussion focuses on other dimensions. Wars are expensive. Someone always benefits financially. Looking at who benefits is not conspiracy theorizing — it's basic political economy.

## The financial mechanism, openly described

What makes the current period unusually clear is that investment analysts describe the mechanism in plain language because their clients need to understand it to make money.

One analysis of the largest US defense contractor describes its stock as "literally a barometer of geopolitical tensions." The stock nearly doubled during the Russia-Ukraine war. The company posted record earnings in 2024 as conflicts intensified. In 2024, 71% of the company's \$67 billion in revenues came from US government contracts — meaning American taxpayers funded the majority of the revenue, while the resulting profits flowed primarily to shareholders. In 2022 alone, the company returned \$11 billion to shareholders through buybacks and dividends.

When a new US administration began signaling intentions to end the Ukraine and Gaza conflicts in 2025, defense stocks fell. Markets read peace as bad for defense industry profits and repriced the stocks accordingly. This is not a moral observation about defense companies. It is a market mechanism that anyone with a brokerage account can observe.

The same pattern operates in other industries. Oil and gas company stocks track resource access. Pharmaceutical company stocks track regulatory and patent regimes. Tech company stocks track antitrust and content moderation policy. In each case, the companies have substantial financial incentive to influence the policies that determine their profits. They do so through campaign contributions, lobbying, post-government employment, and political advertising. The arrangement is legal, openly disclosed in some respects, and pervasive.

## The political capture, named precisely

A specific feature of US foreign policy worth naming: AIPAC (the American Israel Public Affairs Committee) is one of the most effective lobbying organizations in US politics. Its political action committees have spent hundreds of millions of dollars in recent election cycles supporting candidates aligned with the current Israeli government's positions and opposing candidates critical of those positions. AIPAC operates legally and discloses its spending. AIPAC is not a stand-in for Jewish people, who hold a wide range of views on Israeli government policy and many of whom (including major Jewish-American organizations like J Street, IfNotNow, and Jewish Voice for Peace) explicitly oppose AIPAC's positions.

The fund's concern with AIPAC isn't about its members' identity or religion. It's about the structural problem of a foreign country's government effectively shaping another country's foreign policy through domestic lobbying. This problem is not unique to AIPAC. Saudi Arabia spends substantial sums lobbying US foreign policy. The UAE does. Taiwan does. Cuba's exile community does in the opposite direction. The structural problem is that foreign governments and their domestic allies shape US foreign policy at the expense of US voters who have no say in foreign elections. And the same problem operates in reverse: US foreign policy shapes other countries' politics without those countries' citizens having any vote in US elections.

The principle the fund supports: a country's foreign policy should be shaped by its own citizens, in accordance with democratic accountability and international law. Foreign governments influencing each other's domestic politics through lobbying networks corrupts the principle. This is true whether the foreign influence comes from Israel, Saudi Arabia, China, Russia, the European Union, or anyone else. The fund's structural commitment to staying out of all of this — out of defense contracting, out of foreign-policy lobbying, out of resource extraction in countries that haven't consented — is what makes the fund different from the existing actors who profit from the current arrangement.

## What the fund does about all this

The fund exists because most people want a different relationship with the global economy than the one they currently have. They want fair wages for the work they do. They want goods and services they can afford. They want their savings to grow rather than be eroded by inflation while the wealthy capture all asset appreciation. They want peace with their neighbors abroad. They want their governments to represent them rather than the small number of corporations and foreign interests that fund the political class.

The fund cannot deliver all of this immediately. But it can change the structure of the global economy progressively, over decades, in ways that reduce the leverage of concentrated economic power and increase the participation of ordinary people in productive ownership. The mechanism is what we've described in earlier articles: members contribute \$1 a month, the fund builds productive enterprise in essential industries, members receive dividends, and the cumulative effect is meaningful redistribution of economic ownership.

What the fund offers specifically to someone who has watched the patterns described above and concluded that something needs to change:

A way to opt out of supporting extractive systems with their money. By directing spending toward fund-owned companies as those companies emerge, members direct profits back to themselves and other members rather than to private shareholders who use those profits to fund political capture.

A way to receive ongoing income from the productive economy. The dividend, growing over decades, becomes a meaningful supplemental income for hundreds of millions of households that currently have no productive ownership at all.

A way to support local entrepreneurs and small businesses through fund-aligned banking, fair financing, and the fund's card network that doesn't extract through interest spreads or excessive merchant fees. The fund competes with global corporations while supporting local businesses — exactly the inverse of the populist critique that global institutions destroy local economies.

A way to be part of an institution that explicitly refuses to profit from war or extraction. The fund's exclusion of defense and weapons manufacturing is a structural commitment in its constitutional layer that cannot be overridden by future votes. Similarly with resource extraction: the fund commits to fair pricing in the value chain from extraction to consumer, leaving the actual extraction to whatever model a resource-rich country chooses for itself (citizen-owned national companies, where citizens decide to organize that way; or licensed private extraction with government royalties, where countries have chosen that path). The fund operates in the downstream value chain — refining, manufacturing, distribution, retail — and operates fairly so that the value created flows to workers, suppliers, and members rather than being concentrated.

A way to support an independent media presence eventually. As the fund grows, it will need its own media organization — not propaganda, but genuine journalism with the structural independence that comes from being citizen-owned rather than oligarch-owned. Most major media in democratic countries are currently owned by concentrated capital with documented financial interests in particular policy outcomes; the fund's eventual media arm operates from a different structural position.

This is what people receive when they join. The rest of this article addresses what happens next — how the fund actually competes with established private firms in real markets, what advantages it has, what disadvantages it faces, and what "winning" actually looks like.

## The four reasons people join (and stay)

The strength of the fund's appeal comes from four reasons that overlap and reinforce each other. No single one of them carries the case alone, but together they explain why someone would sign up and stay signed up for decades.

The first reason is economic. Dividends begin from the moment the fund has acquired its first companies and produced its first quarter of profit — not 30 years from now. In Year 1, with the fund's portfolio still small and membership growing, the per-member dividend is modest — under \$1 across all scenarios. By Year 5, with multiple acquired companies in essential goods and the digital banking arm generating fees, the dividend has grown to roughly \$7-8 per member annually — meaningful for households in lower-income countries where this represents a week's wages, and a useful supplement elsewhere. By Year 10, the dividend reaches roughly \$25-33 per member annually, becoming a substantial event in poorer countries while remaining a modest supplement in wealthy ones. The first decade is the foundational period; the transformational dividend trajectory unfolds across the following two decades. By Year 20, with the fund's compounding capital base and expanded operations, dividends approach a significant share of monthly income for many households in developing countries. By Year 30, in the steady state, the per-member dividend reaches roughly \$400-600 annually — substantial supplemental income everywhere and transformational where wages are lowest.

Over the full 30-year trajectory, a member contributing \$1 a month accumulates \$360 in contributions and receives, in cumulative dividends, somewhere between \$5,000 and \$10,000. The ratio is 14x to 28x. The long-term economics are favorable, the early years are not empty, and the cost of participation is trivial throughout.

A second economic dimension worth naming: the fund reinvests the *difference* between competitor margin and 10% margin from acquired companies into expansion, as described in the previous article. This reinvestment is invisible to members because their dividends are calculated at the 10% margin equivalent regardless. Members don't experience the margin trajectory as a cut. The reinvestment of *member dividends themselves* — asking members to forgo some dividend to accelerate fund expansion — is a separate decision and is voted on by members. So is any proposal to increase the monthly contribution beyond \$1. Members decide these things. Fund leadership cannot unilaterally redirect member dividends or raise the contribution rate. The fund's growth comes from member contributions plus the margin-trajectory reinvestment plus whatever members vote to add. The default is that members receive their dividends; raising contributions or reinvesting dividends requires their consent.

The second reason is that the cost is so low it doesn't require strong belief. Movements that ask people to make significant sacrifices in pursuit of large changes generally fail because most people don't make significant sacrifices for political abstractions. The fund inverts this: the sacrifice is small enough that broad agreement with the mission is sufficient. People who think "this is probably a good idea" can act on that thought without performing any calculation.

The third reason is what membership actually represents. A fund member is a co-owner of productive economic assets. Not a customer of a service, not a member of an organization, not a donor to a cause — a co-owner. This produces psychological effects that pure economic analysis underestimates. When a fund member purchases from a fund-owned company, the transaction isn't between a stranger and a corporation. It's between a member and a company that member partially owns. Over time, identity attaches to the membership in ways that make it stickier than economic calculation alone would predict.

The fourth reason — and based on the polling above, this is probably the strongest reason for joining in the first place — is the political and moral satisfaction of finally having something concrete to do about a pattern most people have spent decades watching get worse. The 2008 financial crisis showed the system was structurally tilted toward concentrated wealth. The Panama Papers showed how the wealthy avoid the rules that apply to everyone else. The COVID period showed billionaire wealth flowing up in real time. The Pandora Papers showed political and economic elites are the same class. The wars since 2022 have shown defense profits compounding while international law breaks down. The recent Trump-Musk arrangement showed billionaires running government agencies investigating their own companies. Most people have concluded the system needs to change. Most lack a mechanism for action. The fund offers one. The cost is \$1 a month. The mechanism scales because billions of small contributions add up to meaningful economic ownership. The action is real, not symbolic.

A useful frame from a recent Oxfam report: "Being economically poor creates hunger. Being politically poor creates anger." The fund addresses both. The dividend reduces economic poverty over time. The ownership reduces political poverty immediately, because members are co-owners of productive enterprise with voting rights in fund governance.

## What members preferentially buy from fund companies, and what they don't

The fund's economic engine depends on members shifting some of their spending toward fund-owned companies as those companies emerge. The honest version of this story varies sharply by purchase category.

For routine purchases of essential goods — food, basic medicines, household products, basic consumer staples — the bias is strong and operates almost unconsciously. The fund-owned product is comparable in quality, often cheaper because of the margin trajectory described in the previous article, and the member knows their purchase ultimately benefits them and other members. A member buying soap or bread doesn't need to think about it.

For considered purchases — appliances, vehicles, financial services, insurance, healthcare coverage — the bias is moderate and requires the fund product to be genuinely competitive. A member won't choose the fund's mortgage if it's clearly worse than a competitor's, won't choose the fund's car if it's clearly inferior. The membership tilts the decision when products are similar. It does not override the decision when products are different.

For aspirational and discretionary purchases — premium consumer electronics, luxury goods, status items, entertainment, high-end fashion — the bias is weak. A member who can afford an expensive watch buys it for reasons that have nothing to do with where the manufacturer's profits go.

The strategic alignment here is favorable. The fund's competitive strength is exactly where consumer bias is strongest: essentials, basic services, banking, healthcare. The fund's weakness — aspirational and premium products — is exactly where consumer bias is weakest, so the weakness doesn't compound.

One critical caveat: the consumer bias requires the fund product to actually be good. If members try fund bread and find it inferior, the bias evaporates quickly. The fund cannot coast on ideology. Operational excellence in producing competitive goods is the precondition for the bias to do its work.

## Why workers and entrepreneurs choose fund-owned companies

The fund recruits workers and talent the same way any large employer does — by offering pay, benefits, working conditions, career growth, and culture that compete with alternatives. The fund has some structural advantages, some structural challenges, and some neutral elements.

For ordinary workers, the fund pays market rates. There is no pay-ratio compression mechanism. The fund needs to recruit good people across the global economy and the only way to do that consistently is to pay what the market requires. The fund's structural advantages operate alongside competitive compensation rather than as substitutes for it: better job security in downturns because patient capital reduces pressure for layoffs; better working conditions on average because mission alignment with worker welfare produces operational outcomes; some sense of ownership because workers are also fund members.

For top talent — engineers, scientists, executives, designers, researchers — the fund competes for a smaller pool of highly skilled people whose compensation expectations are extreme. The fund pays market salaries here as well, with one important addition: the royalties pathway. A scientist who develops a drug, a designer who creates a successful product, a software architect who builds a major fund system receives ongoing royalties on the revenue their work generates. This is closer to direct compensation for value created than conventional stock options. The fund competes successfully for talent in most categories but accepts that it will lose some senior people to competitors whose compensation packages it cannot match.

For entrepreneurs, the fund offers something genuinely distinctive. The bank, as described in the previous article, operates as an equity investor with buyback rights rather than as an interest-charging lender. A small business owner who would currently take a 12% bank loan can instead take fund capital in exchange for an equity share with buyback rights. The terms are structurally better; risk is shared rather than transferred entirely to the borrower. For entrepreneurs building businesses that fit the fund's ecosystem, the bank provides zero-cost capital.

A category worth naming directly: people who would join fund-owned companies specifically because of mission alignment. This is real for some workers and irrelevant for others. A pharmaceutical researcher who wants to develop affordable medicines for billions may find a fund-owned pharma company more attractive than a private one even at slightly lower pay. The mission-motivated minority is real and matters for certain roles, though the fund doesn't assume this is most workers.

## The fund's structural advantages, honestly

With the political and moral case established and the participation reasons mapped, we can finally address the question the article's title raises: how does the fund actually compete with established private firms that have decades of head start?

The fund has four structural advantages. Each is real, each has limits, and the combination produces a competitive position that varies by industry.

The first advantage is patient capital with no quarterly pressure. Public companies are bent by the requirement to deliver returns every three months. Private equity is bent by the requirement to exit within 5-10 years. The fund has effectively no time-horizon constraints. Its members aren't trying to exit; its capital comes from contributions and reinvested profits that don't require returns on any specific schedule. The advantage is largest in capital-intensive industries (steel, cement, energy infrastructure, advanced pharmaceutical research) where 15-30 year payback periods are common, and in any industry where the right move requires waiting through several years of competitive pressure.

The second advantage is the dividend feedback loop creating collective consumer preference. This isn't transaction-level — the dividend a member receives from any single purchase is pennies. It's collective. As more members direct more spending to fund-owned companies, fund profits grow, dividends grow, more members join, and the cycle reinforces. The mechanism is slow-acting but compounds over decades. It operates most strongly in essentials and basic services.

The third advantage is mission flexibility in pricing and operations. The fund can operate at 10% margins on essentials because its members benefit from both lower prices and stable dividends. Private competitors operating at 20-30% margins on the same essentials cannot easily compress margins because their shareholders demand the higher returns. Over time, the fund can underprice private competitors in essential categories while still generating returns that benefit its members. The constitutional cap on essential goods margins makes this commitment credible to consumers in ways that private commitments to "fair pricing" never are.

The fourth advantage is structural alignment with workers and suppliers. The fund's workers are also fund members. The fund's customers are also fund members. The fund's suppliers operate as partners rather than as parties to be squeezed. The result is lower turnover, better supplier relationships, fewer labor disputes, and more reliable supply chains. None of this is dramatic on any given day. Over a decade, it produces a measurable operational advantage. Mondragón's 65-year history demonstrates this works at scale.

## Where the fund's advantages don't apply

The honest acknowledgment that defines the article's title: the fund wins some markets, not all.

First, the fund probably never matches the brand value, design sensibility, and product excellence of premium consumer categories. Apple, Hermès, Patek Philippe, Ferrari, Rolex — these represent 50-100 years of accumulated taste, craftsmanship, and brand mystique. The fund's eventual presence in luxury serves different purposes (capturing wealth that would otherwise flow entirely to private capital, supporting design talent through royalties), but doesn't displace the established brands in their premium niches.

Second, the fund is slower than private competitors in fast-moving markets. Software, fashion, entertainment, consumer technology — these markets reward speed, risk tolerance, and rapid pivots that the fund's governance and patient capital orientation make harder. Private startups iterate faster than fund-owned ventures probably ever will. The fund's strategy in software, building on open-source infrastructure rather than competing with proprietary giants directly, reflects this reality.

Third, the fund is vulnerable to coordinated political and economic attack. Private competitors with hundreds of billions in market capitalization can lose money for years to drive out competitors. They can lobby regulators. They can fund political campaigns. They can run information operations. They can use their home governments' financial infrastructure to constrain the fund's operations. The fund has structural defenses — its multi-country digital banking presence, its constitutional commitments, its large and politically engaged membership base — but the defenses are imperfect. The fund will lose some battles. It needs to win enough to keep growing.

## What winning actually means

The fund doesn't need to win every market. It needs to win enough to generate the dividends, redistribute manufacturing employment, establish wage and price norms that private competitors have to respond to, and demonstrate that an alternative economic model is operationally viable at scale.

Quantitatively, the fund's target is the 12-18% of global productive activity described in the previous article — significant but not dominant. Within those share levels, "winning" means specific things readers can verify:

Members receive meaningful dividends. By Year 30, the per-member dividend reaches roughly \$400-600 annually in the steady state — substantial supplemental income for a household and transformational in lower-income countries.

Manufacturing employment redistributes. The Informal Insertion scenario from Part 3 produces tens of millions of new formal manufacturing jobs in regions currently dominated by informal labor.

Essential goods prices fall. Drug prices, basic food prices, basic consumer staples prices, and basic financial service costs decline in fund-served markets as the margin trajectory compresses competitor pricing.

Workers gain bargaining power. Fund-owned companies establish wage floors that private competitors have to respond to. Even at 20% market share, the fund's wage norms reshape what workers in the other 80% of the market can credibly demand.

Citizens gain participation. Members exercise voting rights in fund governance — choosing acquisitions, ratifying constitutional changes, electing the bodies that oversee fund operations. The participation is real, not symbolic.

These are real outcomes affecting billions of people. They don't require the fund to displace all of capitalism. They require the fund to demonstrate that an alternative model can co-exist with private capital, compete with it in the markets where the fund's advantages apply, and progressively shift the structure of the global economy toward fairness without violence.

This is the honest version of what winning means. Partial wins are still transformative. The fund operating at 12-18% of global production with the characteristics described above changes the world for billions of people without requiring revolution, without requiring war, without requiring the destruction of existing institutions, and without requiring everyone to agree. People who don't want to join can continue not joining. People who don't want to buy from fund companies can continue not buying. The fund's success is compatible with continued private capital, continued state operation in legitimate state domains, continued local entrepreneurship, and continued diversity of economic models across countries.

What the fund cannot coexist with is the current pattern in which a small concentrated group of economic actors uses political capture to extract from everyone else and profit from the wars that result when extraction meets resistance. The fund's success means that pattern progressively loses its grip — not by being defeated militarily or politically, but by being competed with at sufficient scale that its returns are no longer extraordinary enough to justify the political costs of maintaining it.
